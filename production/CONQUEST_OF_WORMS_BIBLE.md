# CONQUEST OF WORMS — Story Bible for Adaptation

**Source read:** the four Reboot issues only, at `G:\My Drive\Rotten Sewer Productions\Comics\Conquest of worms\Conquest of Worms Reboot\` — Issue 1 (20pp, Apr 2022), issue 2 (24pp, Sept 2023), Issue 3 *"Run Rabbit, Run"* (24pp, `Run Rabbit Run Final.pdf`, 2024/2025), issue 4 (23 scanned pages, 2025, **drawn but unwritten**).
**Not read, by the author's instruction:** the `conquest of worms 68` pilot, `old comics\ww1` (a 2015 pre-canon experiment), `misc`.
**Companion document:** `production/CONQUEST_OF_WORMS_AUTHOR_SYNOPSIS.md` carries the author's verbatim account of the spine and of Issue 4's unwritten script. This bible reports what is **on the page**. Where page and synopsis disagree, both are stated and neither is smoothed over.
**Page numbering:** Issues 1–3 by printed page including covers. Issue 4 by scan number `fya.01`–`fya.23` (unnumbered, no cover drawn).
**Written 2026-09-09.** All quotes transcribed from the art; the author's spelling and punctuation are kept verbatim, typos included.

## 1 · PREMISE

A seventeen-year-old from Minden, Louisiana signs a Selective Service card in August 1967 and by November is a replacement in the 101st Airborne, humping an M79 through the highlands. He carries his grandfather's First World War journal in his ruck and reads it aloud to the squad in the dark. The journal is not a comfort. It is a record of the same war, one generation back and one continent over — the same mud, the same lying officers, the same boy who breaks, the same dead who will not shut up — and the man who wrote it made one merciful decision in a shell-torn French field that is, half a century later, hunting his grandson through the elephant grass. Meanwhile the replacement who came in alongside Michael starts taking ears, then a head, then more, and the horror in this book is never a monster: it is a person going, drawn as worms because that is what a mind looks like when it is being eaten from the inside. **Conquest of Worms** is a documentary war comic about grunts doing grunt work — burning shit barrels, waiting in an ambush, filing across a stream — that periodically opens onto the inside of somebody's head and finds it full of maggots.

The title is the thesis: the war eats down the generations.

## 2 · CHARACTERS (REBOOT CANON)

### VIETNAM

**MICHAEL LEE CRAWFORD** — protagonist and first-person narrator. Private, rifle company, 101st Airborne. Enters Issue 1 p3. Carries an M79 grenade launcher ("Thumper") and his grandfather's journal.

Draft card, Issue 1 title page (p2), drawn as a period Selective Service registration certificate:
> **MICHAEL LEE CRAWFORD** · Selective Service No. **29 22 49 281** · Date of birth **August 14, 1950** · Place of birth **Minden, Louisiana** · Eyes **Blue** · Hair **Brown** · Height **6'2"** · Weight **155 lbs** · Date of registration **August 17, 1967**

He is seventeen at registration and the card is drawn accurately enough that the arithmetic works. Around it on the title page is a collage of **real, older draft cards** — one legibly for a man in Pittsburgh, Pa. — so the motif is established on page 2 of the Reboot: *the paperwork of conscription as design language.*

Voice — Issue 1 p4, the opening narration, and the register the whole book is written in:
> "I've been in Vietnam for a little over two months now. Still a green grunt fresh off the American assembly line. 16 months of drills & instruction to prepare me for the ultimate sacrifice."

By Issue 2 his narration has loosened into spoken vernacular, with "ya know?" and dropped auxiliaries — the author's ear improving in real time across the run. He vomits after his first ambush (Issue 2 p22) and is not spared for it: *"Levi, Take care of this mess Crawford just made of himself. Get em squared away and the men back on the move."*

Michael is the one who half-believes. Issue 3 p16, of his great-grandmother the medium and the crater:
> "That's the story I've always been told... after reading my grandfathers journal, I've gotta believe its more true than not. We never got a chance to meet."
> "I never did get to ask my grandfather about what exactly happened to him out there in that bomb crater."

**LEVI, called "EYES"** — Michael's closest friend in the squad, present from Issue 1 p8. The squad's read on the situation comes through him. Issue 2 p4:
> "Man, Michael I gotta tell ya. Ever since Sgt. Maddox ate that bullet the other week, and McCleary has taken over. Its been like were an actual unit. Cpl. Champs has been doing his best but sometimes I just don't know..."

**SGT. MADDOX** — squad leader in Issue 1. Blunt and correct. Issue 1 p6, to Michael:
> "Lose that Thumper, boy! Grab a rifle, plenty to choose from!!"

**Killed between issues** — shot, offhand, reported in a line of dialogue: *"ate that bullet the other week"* (Issue 2 p4). A death that happens in the gutter, not on the page. The book's most characteristic move.

**SGT. McCLEARY** — takes over the squad after Maddox. Competent; the squad notices the difference. Gives the Gus introduction speech, Issue 2 p4:
> "As for the rest of you, I expect you to treat Gus as if he was one of your own, men. Until Brass can get an actual spot for him, he's going to be floating around where ever we need him to be."

**CHAMPS** — the officer figure, and the **rank is genuinely inconsistent in the source.** Issue 1 p15 presents him as a Lieutenant. Issue 2's title page carries an author's erratum:
> "We will have to make an amendment to Issue 1. While in the woods having a nightmare, Michael responds to Corporal Champs as Lieutenant. That was a mistake."

Then Issue 3 calls him **"Lt. Champs"** repeatedly (pp. 14, 18, 19). **Unresolved drift in the source. Flag it rather than picking one.**

Champs' voice, Issue 1 p15, pushing pep pills — his own bitten fingernails drawn in close-up in the same panel:
> "Charlie's gonna be real sorry to hear about your noggin, Cupcake. I don't have time for excuses. Pop some of these mamas."
> "Take the fuckin pill, Crenshaw."

He is the man who beats Eugene and drives him out (Issue 3 p19):
> "Get up, you sick fuck. Fight me like a man."
> "Get the fuck outta my AO, Gus!"

**"GUS" — EUGENE** — the other replacement, arrives Issue 2 p4, unassigned and floating. The synopsis names him Eugene; **every drawn page calls him Gus.** He is eager and out of place from his first line:
> "Need a hand, Private? Your boots look soaked"

and Michael's read on him is instant (Issue 2 p4):
> "How the hell does a guy like that even end up in a combat frontline unit?"

His arc is section 5. **His whole decline occupies eleven drawn pages.**

**ANGUS** — squad member, named Issue 2 p4. Little else on the page.

**CRENSHAW** — named once, Issue 1 p15, as the man refusing the pill: *"Oh, no that's okay.. I'll just make some cof—"*. Whether this is a distinct soldier or a slip is **not resolvable from the page**; it reads as a separate man declining while Champs is working on Michael.

**THE UNNAMED VETERANS** — Issue 2 p23, the men who normalise the ears. Their dialogue is the book's moral centre of gravity and should be preserved verbatim if anything is:
> "I think Gus is harmless, Man. This is war. We gotta take trophies and show those little men that were just as tough as they are. I don't think I'll start wearing ears any time soon but I get what He is doing."
> "Sometimes taking a little piece of Charlie home is the only we HQ can even confirm a kill, Baby!"
> "Search and Destroy! Ive been here for 7 months and its good to finally get some for real. Tired of burning shit barels"
> "When you been out here as long as some of us have, you do anything to feel alive. Gus is just doing it right away, it looks like."

Against them, one dissenting voice: *"How can we just allow Gus to do something like that? ... He just seemed like a cool guy but now I don't even know."*

**THE VIETNAMESE VENDOR** — Issue 3 p8, in a village market, sells Gus a protective charm and delivers the first news of the sniper: a ghost "out looking for young American Soldier." Unnamed. She is the only Vietnamese character in the four issues who speaks.

**THE NURSE (Issue 4)** — **drawn but unnamed and unlettered.** Dark curly hair, freckles, fatigues; a run of panels in the aid station and ward (`fya.14`, `fya.17`). She is the gentlest drawing in the issue and the book does not leave her alone in it — see the contamination note in §5. **The synopsis's "smoozing this nurse" does not read as charm on the page.** In `fya.14` a soldier's arm is across her with his **hand at her waist**, and her face is apprehensive, not pleased. Whatever the words turn out to be, the drawing is uncomfortable.

**THE MAJOR / THE MAN WHO IS SHOT (Issue 4)** — **the shooting IS drawn.** `fya.12`, lower left: a hand-lettered **"BLAM"** in jagged caps, a small crouched figure in an undershirt firing a pistol at point-blank range into a standing man in a **soft bush hat**, the head bursting in a spray of ink, a mess tray flying, men scattering out of the panel. `fya.13` opens on two soldiers standing over the body in the mud. **The victim's rank is not legible on the page** — no insignia reads at this size — so "the major" is the synopsis's word, not the art's. The theft of the pistol is not itself drawn; the panel above shows the crowd standing over the crouched man, then BLAM.

**THE SKULL-FACED SNIPER** — section 5.

### THE WW1 THREAD (separate cast — do not merge)

**LOUIE (LOUIS)** — Michael's grandfather; the WW1 point-of-view character, writing the journal Michael reads. **French**, from Aix-en-Provence, seventeen when he goes to war in April 1915. Named for a king. Issue 2 p7:
> "Louis, even kings face their share of challenges. But with that determination of yours, who knows? Maybe you'll rewrite history in your own way."

His mother is a **medium**, locally famous — Issue 3 p3. Her parting words to him, quoted twice (Issue 2 p7; Issue 3 p2 recap):
> "Inside you are two dogs, one red and one black."

And Louie's own gloss, Issue 3 p3:
> "I assumed she was parting some special words to me, but so far all I've seen is death."
> "But when I look over at this guy here, all I can see inside of him are the worms and mud!"

The journal's closing note in Issue 2 p7 is the thesis of the entire title, stated once and never repeated:
> "And as the rain falls outside, I can't help but think of the worms, burrowing beneath the surface, carrying on despite everything that is going on. Life continues..."

Louie's lettering is a **cursive handwritten face**, distinct from the typewriter font used in Vietnam. The two eras are typographically separated as well as tonally.

**PIERRE** — the timid one. Bullied, breaks down, and is killed going over the top (Issue 2 pp. 8–15). This is the author's "pussy boy who looks just like eugnue." **On the page, the resemblance is a drawing decision and is never stated in text** — both Pierre and Gus are drawn young, gaunt, wide-eyed, with dotted freckles across the cheekbones and a wide open mouth. Compatible with the intent; not signposted. Report it as an *artist's rhyme*, not a plot point.

Pierre's voice, Issue 2 p10, refusing to keep collecting the dead:
> "I can't take it anymore! ... We're surrounded by death, by this hell! Why should we be any different? Why should we live while they lie here, forgotten?"

And Issue 2 p10, in the trench beside a wormy corpse:
> "Dear GOD! Were all going to die!! Momma!! Get me out of this hell hole!!"

He is pistol-whipped for trying to climb the ladder (a **KRAKK** sound effect) and dies with rosary beads in his hand (Issue 2 p15).

**GASTON** — the older man who cannot stand Pierre; the bully. Issue 2 p9: *"You filthy little worm!"* — the book's only use of "worm" as an insult, and it lands on the Eugene-double. Per the synopsis he survives into the French Foreign Legion in Indochina; **no page in the four issues shows that**, and neither does any page show the woman it is said to drive into the revolution. She is intent, not art.

**LIEUTENANT DURAND** — the officer in the trench and in the shell hole. Hard-mouthed. Issue 2 p8, on a corpse dug out of the trench wall:
> "Oh! I see you found one of the former residents of the Trench! Ha, don't mind him. He is the least of your worries now, Pierre."
> "Pierre, did you make a matey?"

Issue 2 p10, after the ladder incident:
> "Disobey my orders again, Pierre and I will shoot you myself."

**Durand is the answer the author could not remember.** Issue 3 pp. 3–7: Louie is in the crater with him for hours. Durand says (p4):
> "Just play dead like the rest of us in this hole!!!"
> — "Huh? Dead?"
> — "What do you mean, We?"

Louie carries him back. At the aid station, Issue 3 p7, a nurse says it out loud:
> "THIS man?! This man has clearly been dead out in the field for months."

**The shell-hole companion is Lt. Durand, and the nurse is an outside witness.** Do not take the synopsis's uncertainty as canon; the page settles it.

**THE YOUNG GERMAN** — Issue 3 p5. Louie has him at the end of a rifle in the open. Durand shouts *"Kill him!!"* Louie says one word:
> "Run..."

The German boy is drawn with a **large, deliberate scar down one cheek** — a mark of identity applied in the panel where he is spared. This is the hinge of the whole series and it is the source of the issue's title. **The pages do not state, anywhere, that this boy becomes the SS officer who burns the Russian.** That chain exists in the author's account only. The scar is the pages' one piece of supporting evidence and it is circumstantial.

**PLISSE / GOSLIN** — two further poilus named in passing in Issue 2 p7's journal text; no arc drawn.

## 4 · TWO TIMELINES, TWO GEOGRAPHIES, AND THE CUT-MAP

### Vietnam thread

- **November 1967.** Stated flatly on Issue 2's title page: *"The story picks back up November, 1967."* Corroborated inside the fiction at Issue 3 p20: *"Im not trying to die before thanksgiving."*
- Michael is **"a little over two months"** in-country at Issue 1 p4, which places Issue 1 around September–October 1967. A soldier at the firebase in Issue 1 p19 says *"Only 320 days left for ya?"* — 45 days in-country, not 60. **Minor internal inconsistency; report, do not fix.**
- **Unit:** 101st Airborne. The Screaming Eagle patch is drawn on the shoulder (Issue 1 p6). Consistent with the author's stated research contact with "No Slack," 2/327 Infantry.
- **AO:** the **"Ashu Valley"** — the author's spelling of A Shau — named in radio traffic at Issue 1 p8. Highlands, triple canopy, elephant grass, monsoon. Firebase with sandbag revetments, quonset huts, a mortar pit and a watchtower (drawn in overhead at Issue 3 p11). A rear-area **HQ base camp** with power lines, deuce-and-a-halfs and an airfield appears in Issue 4.
- **Callsigns and units on the page:** VF-GRAPE · Pink Panther · 1-1 Actual (Issue 1 p8) · Viper 2-1 and Viper 2-2, the fast movers (Issue 1 p12).
- **Real operations referenced:** **Operation Wandering Soul** — the psy-op ghost tapes — explained by a soldier in Issue 3 p16: *"fly around real low over the trees at night, playing this spooky ass recordings... Victor Charlie really believes in ghosts. And the army is using their own bedtime stories against 'em."* Also **"Search and Destroy"** (Issue 2 p23).

### WW1 thread

- **April 1915**, Aix-en-Provence; Louie leaves for Paris (Issue 2 p6). **France, 1915** is captioned outright at Issue 3 p3.
- **Notre-Dame de Lorette** is named at Issue 2 p11 — the Second Battle of Artois, May 1915. That fixes the WW1 AO as precisely as "Ashu Valley" fixes the Vietnam one.
- Unit not given beyond that Louie is a French infantryman under Lt. Durand; Adrian helmets and horizon-blue kit are drawn.
- Trench life: dugouts marked **"HOME"** in chalk, corpses in the revetment, a burial-and-dogtag detail over both French and German dead, a shell hole, own artillery falling short (Issue 2 p16: *"These are our own shells!"*), and going over the top under a German machine gun.

### How the cuts are made

- **Into WW1 (Issue 2 p5) — on the object.** Michael takes the journal out of his ruck and tells the squad what it is; the transition is the physical book being opened. His mother gave it to him before Basic — *"Whatever that was supposed to mean."* The cut is diegetic and gentle.
- **Out of WW1 (Issue 2 p16→17) — on a shock.** French artillery falls on French troops; the next page is a **full-page Vietnam splash of a skeleton in web gear holding an M16, standing in the jungle beside Michael's face.** No caption. The rhyme is *the dead of one war standing up in the next*, and it is made entirely in pictures.
- **Issue 3 opens cold in 1915** with no framing at all, then hard-cuts at p7→8 from a French nurse saying the officer has been dead for months, straight into a Vietnamese market where a vendor is selling a charm against a ghost. **Both halves of that cut are about a dead man who is still operating.** This is the most deliberate rhyme in the book.
- **Issue 3 p16 makes the parallel spoken rather than drawn:** the squad discusses Wandering Soul and Michael's medium great-grandmother in the same conversation. The army weaponising ghosts and Michael's family believing in them, on one page.

**On "parallels between the characters across time":** what the pages support is a parallel of **situation and face**, not of protagonist. Michael and Louie are not built as echoes of each other — Michael is passive and observant, Louie is a boy who acts once, decisively. The strongest drawn parallel is **Pierre ↔ Gus/Eugene**: the same physical type, both the squad's weak link, both destroyed. The author intends this. The text never says it. **Do not decide it; it is a face.**

## 5 · THE WORMS, AND THE HORROR RULE

**The author's rule, verbatim:** *"any horror gore scene in the comic is supposed to be representative of the psychological state of the person expericing the vision"* and *"the worms show up in both wars because its more psychological than metaphorical but also like war is gorey."*

The pages hold to this rigorously. **Every horror image in the Reboot belongs to somebody**, and it measures that person, not the world. That is the working rule for any adaptation.

### "Whose vision" — a per-character horror inventory

**MICHAEL.** His horror is **skulls, graves and being touched by the dead.** It starts on page 3 of the whole Reboot and it never escalates.
- **Issue 1 p3** — faces inside helmets with **worms crawling on the skin and small ring-shaped pocks around the eye**, one helmet band lettered **"FUCK PEACE"** beside a peace sign. Broken by a voice: *"MICHAEL, ARE YOU LISTENING?"* A daydream, in a briefing. **The pock motif starts here, on page 3 of the whole Reboot.**
- **Issue 1 p15** — a graveyard, a corpse rising out of the ground, worms. *"No! No! AHH! GET OFF ME!!"* He is asleep in the woods and wakes to Champs.
- **Issue 1 p16** — the honest, small one, on night watch under flares: *"I swear I saw skeletons out in the bush."* **This is the book's calibration point.** It is a frightened man's report, not an event.
- **Issue 2 p17** — the skeleton in web gear holding an M16, full page, no words, immediately after the WW1 friendly-fire page.

Michael's visions are **static and passive**: things that stand there and look back. He never sees himself in them.

**LOUIE.** His horror is **the dead being companionable** — worse, and quieter.
- **Issue 2 pp. 8–10** — corpses in the trench wall treated as furniture; a wormy skull propped beside Pierre while Pierre screams for his mother; a starburst caption reading **"SAVE ME."**
- **Issue 2 p13** — a German machine gunner in a pickelhaube drawn as a skull, framed in a hard iris with the muzzle pointing at the reader. The enemy as a face.
- **Issue 3 pp. 3–7** — **the central WW1 horror: hours of conversation with an officer who has been dead for months.** Uniquely in the book, this one is **corroborated by an outside witness** — the nurse sees the corpse. So Louie's vision is either a hallucination that carried a real body, or the dead talked. **The text does not resolve it and this bible will not.**

Louie's visions are **interactive**. Michael is looked at; Louie is talked to. That difference is the clearest characterisation the two men get.

**EUGENE / GUS.** His horror is **appetite**, and it is the only inventory in the book that escalates.
- **Issue 2 p22** — his first kill, praised. *"Good work, Gus. Another one for the books."* His thought balloon, alone in the panel: **"Is that what were supposed to be doing?"**
- **Issue 2 p23 — the very next page** — he is already taking ears. **The gap between "is this right?" and mutilation is one page turn.** That is the most important structural fact about him.
- **Issue 3 p9** — the ears have become a necklace worn openly in camp. *"God, what a fuckin freak."*
- **Issue 3 p10** — he is hiding something in his bag.
- **Issue 3 pp. 12–13, 17** — he lags behind the file and is found sitting in the grass playing with a corpse.
- **Issue 3 p18 — the escalation the synopsis understates.** Gus is **eating a human arm, on the page, in front of an officer**, and he offers some: *"Hey, Lt. Champs! Just in time. Do you want some of this, its still good!!"* **The cannibalism in Issue 4 is not a rumour. It is a witnessed fact from Issue 3, and the squad saw it.**
- **Issue 3 p19** — Champs beats him. *"Get the fuck outta my AO, Gus!"* — and *"...Leave the bag."*
- **Issue 4 `fya.09` — his splash.** A full page in which the entire jungle is made of worms and skulls, Hueys firing overhead, and grave markers hand-lettered **KILLED · ROTTEN · EATEN · KILLED**. It is by a wide margin the wormiest page in the Reboot, and it is Eugene's. The rule holds perfectly: **the man with the worst mind gets the worst page.**
- **Issue 4 `fya.11`** — he is eating outdoors with two men standing over him. The man at left is drawn with a **damaged or scarred face**; the man at right, in a wide-brim bush hat, is drawn hollow and skeletal. **These are men in a chow line, rendered as corpses.** The vision has stopped waiting for nightfall and is now overwriting the people in front of him.
- **Issue 4 `fya.12` — the killing, and the book's rule at maximum.** A crowd stands over him. Then, in the lower left, **"BLAM"** and a man's head coming apart. And immediately beside it, taking half the page, **the largest horror image in the sequence — an enormous face, mouth open in a howl, eyes wide, and WEEPING.** *(Corrected 2026-09-09 on the author's own word: "hes crying not melting." Verified at full magnification — the marks fall in wavy columns from the lower lids down both cheeks, several ending in a rounded bead. The jaw, nose and cheekbones all hold their structure. Nothing on this face is coming apart. The separate small **ring-shaped pocks** sit on the cheek among the tear tracks, which is what I collapsed the two motifs on.)*

  **This changes what the image means, not just what it is called.** Melting would be a man becoming a monster. Crying is a man staying entirely human while doing something monstrous — present, and knowing exactly what he has just done. **The vision does not follow the act; it arrives with it, and what it measures is grief.** If a horror panel is a readout of a mind, this is the needle going off the dial in the same instant the trigger moves, and the reading it gives is not transformation. It is a boy sobbing.
- **Issue 4 `fya.13`** — the body in the mud; a hand held up with **an object that reads either as an IV bag with a drip line or as a small pouch held up as evidence** (genuinely ambiguous at this resolution, and it matters, because Gus's bag is a plot object); then two vast faces, one snarling with bared teeth, one weeping and covered in ring-shaped pocks; then a ward interior with a man raving on a cot and a shape over him.
- **Issue 4 `fya.18`** — a skull-grin over the medevac loading.

**TWO MOTIFS THAT MUST BE KEPT APART.** They are drawn with different marks and they mean opposite things, and conflating them is how the `fya.12` face got misread:
- **TEARS RUN.** Wavy lines travelling *down* from the lower lid, often ending in a bead. **Grief.** They appear on `fya.12`'s face, on `fya.13`'s weeping face, and on the nurse.
- **POCKS SIT.** Small closed rings and circles resting *on* the skin, going nowhere. **Contamination.** They appear from Issue 1 p3 onward, and on the diseased faces throughout Issue 4.

**THE NURSE.** In `fya.14` her freckles are small dots across the nose and cheek. On the last panel of the page — a huge eye with a **tear** falling, lashes and all — **the marks on the cheek are drawn as closed rings**, the pock motif, not the dots. **The tear and the rings are two separate things on one face, and only the rings are the change.**

*(Offered as the strongest available reading with its ambiguity stated: the final panel could be a different, bandaged face, though the lashes and the eye match hers. The tear alone proves nothing — half the faces in this issue weep.)*

If it is her, it is the most disciplined use of his own rule in the whole run: **the one person being kind to him is being redrawn by the mind looking at her.** She does not decay. The vision reaches her. And on the same page a soldier's hand is at her waist while her face is apprehensive — so what is happening to her in the art and what is happening to her in the story may be the same thing.

**THE SNIPER.** He does not get visions. **He is one** — for everyone else.

**Attribution audit:** I looked specifically for a horror image that cannot be pinned on a character present in the scene. **I did not find one in the four issues.** The two back covers are the only arguable cases, and a cover is authorial by convention. The rule is not broken.

### The worms, literally

They are drawn as ordinary earthworms and maggots, always in mass, and they turn up in **both wars**:
- **WW1:** Issue 2 back cover — a poilu going over the wire whose **entire face is a churning mass of worms**. Issue 3 back cover — worms pouring over a trench parapet beside an abandoned rifle and helmet. Issue 2 pp. 8–10 — worms in the trench dead.
- **Vietnam:** Issue 1 pp. 3, 15, 18 (small ones at the edge of a mud-and-poncho panel). Issue 4 `fya.09`.

**So the worms cross both wars, and this is the thesis made visible.** Two generations, two continents, fifty-two years, the same organism doing the same work under both. Louie's journal states it in words once (Issue 2 p7, quoted above) and then the book never explains it again — it just keeps drawing it.

### Is the horror literal, or is it a man coming apart?

**The text does not decide, and it is careful not to.** What it actually supports:
- Every horror image is attributable to a mind that is present.
- But the book plants **exactly one piece of external corroboration**, and it plants it in the past, in the safest place: the WW1 nurse confirming that Louie's talking officer was a months-old corpse (Issue 3 p7). One witness, one time, one war back.
- And it plants a **cultural** corroboration in the present: Operation Wandering Soul is a real US Army programme that broadcast ghost recordings, and the book puts it on the same page as Michael's medium ancestor (Issue 3 p16). The army itself is in the business of hauntings.

The honest reading: **it is a man coming apart, in a world that has left the door open a crack.** Adapt it that way and you are inside the author's rule. Adapt it as literal monsters and you have made a different book.

### The sniper — every appearance, in order

He is built as a **presence before a person**, across three issues, and this structure is the best thing in the comic for a game.

1. **Issue 3 p8 — as folklore.** A market vendor sells Gus a charm and warns of a ghost "out looking for young American Soldier." No image of him at all.
2. **Issue 3 p13, bottom panel — as a silhouette the squad never sees.** A prone figure in the foreground brush with a scoped rifle, watching Michael's file cross a stream. The squad walks on. **No dialogue, no acknowledgement, no reaction shot.**
3. **Issue 3 p20 — as a fragment.** Extreme close-up: one eye, and a cheek eaten away so the molars show through. Beside it, a scope reticle laid over the departing squad. A face, but only a piece of one.
4. **Issue 3 pp. 21–22 — as a shooter and a taker.** Gunfire as Gus runs; on the last page, his disfigured face looms over panels of Gus being taken. "To be continued..."
5. **Issue 4 `fya.15` — the captivity, drawn.** NVA dragging a prisoner through water; a **bamboo cage** holding a US soldier in a "U.S. ARMY" chest rig, guards outside. **This sequence is not in the author's synopsis at all.**
6. **Issue 4 `fya.20`–`fya.22` — the approach.** Rain over a village; VC porters in conical hats moving a supply column through the palms past a **skull half-buried in the mud**; the column arrives at a dugout whose entrance is **ringed with human skulls**.
7. **Issue 4 `fya.23` — THE REVEAL, and it delivers.** Full page. The dugout's lintel and posts are hung with a **row of captured American M1 helmets**. Inside, a **hammer-and-sickle flag** on the wall. And the man: burned and scarred face, wild hair and beard, sitting in the mouth of the hole with a **scoped bolt-action rifle across his lap**, an open crate with a second rifle and a blade, and a basket of fruit the porters have just brought him.

**The single largest finding in Issue 4, and it is not in the synopsis: the sniper takes trophies too.** Eugene wears a necklace of ears; the sniper hangs a wall of helmets. The book's two monsters are doing the same thing, and the drawn pages set them next to each other without a word of comment. **That is the reveal, and it is entirely visual.**

Also on the page and not in the synopsis: **he is supplied by the local VC**, who carry food to him. He is not a lone spectre — he is an asset somebody feeds.

## 6 · THE TONAL RULE

**The covers state the rule by omission.** Issue 1's cover is two Hueys over elephant grass and a man wading with his rifle held overhead — mountains, no horror. Issue 2's is a combat assault, men coming off the skids. Issue 3's is Vietnam. **No Reboot cover has a worm on it.** Every Reboot **back** cover does — and two of the three back covers are **WW1**. The horror is sold on the way out, not the way in. Against a pilot cover that led with worms and a Claymore, this is a deliberate craft decision and it should be respected in any adaptation's marketing and first hour.

**What runs straight (documentary):**
- Combat assault, LZ prep with det cord, the stick going out (Issue 1 pp. 7–9)
- A hidden multi-barrel AA gun in a village shooting down a Huey — *"What the fuck is that!?" / "BRACE FOR IMPACT!!!!"* (Issue 1 p10)
- Fast movers, napalm, and the canisters drawn with hand-lettered graffiti — **"DUCK + COVER"**, **"FUCK YOU CHARLIE"**, a skull and crossbones (Issue 1 p13)
- A night probe on the perimeter under flares (Issue 1 pp. 16–17)
- Monsoon; the firebase; a card game in a hooch; *"Only 320 days left for ya?"* (Issue 1 p19)
- A silent six-panel ambush wait ending on an eye and an insect on a man's cheek (Issue 2 p18)
- Rocket attack on the firebase, drawn from overhead (Issue 3 p11)
- The rear: burning shit barrels, the chow line, the airfield, the signpost reading **INFIRMARY / HANOI / LONDON / NEW YORK** (Issue 4)
- The WW1 half is equally documentary: burial details, dogtag collection, chalked dugouts, short-falling friendly artillery

**What goes strange:** the four Michael nightmares, the Durand crater, the two back covers, Eugene's splash. That is it. **Roughly one page in eight.** The book is eight-ninths dirt.

**Craft observations worth carrying:**
- **Two lettering faces:** typewriter for Vietnam, cursive for the journal. The eras are legible before you read a word.
- **Two ink registers:** Vietnam is heavy spot black, underground-comix weight. The WW1 pages carry more **grey wash**. Issue 4, still unlettered, is entirely grey marker and brush.
- **The recurring paper motif.** Issue 1's title page is draft cards. Issue 2's is **PS Magazine**-style army maintenance-manual art — *"DRAIN BEFORE SHOOTING"*, *"WHAT TO DO IN A JAM"*, M16A1 immediate action. Issue 3's recap page sits on an army **rabbit-skinning / "Sharpening the Combat Edge"** manual, which is where the title *"Run Rabbit, Run"* comes from; the issue closes with an afterword called **"The Rabbit Lesson."** **Official documents are this book's connective tissue.**
- **Deaths happen in the gutter.** Sgt. Maddox is killed in a subordinate clause. That restraint is the whole tone.

**THE QUESTION FOR THE SUMMONER, stated plainly and not answered here:**

> Is WW1 a **second playable war** — a separate set of levels with French kit, a trench AO, and a spare-or-shoot decision — or is it **narration**: the journal read aloud over the campfire and in loading screens, with WW1 appearing only as art and audio?
>
> These are two different games. The first roughly doubles the art budget (a whole era of weapons, uniforms, terrain and enemies) to serve ~18% of the source's page count. The second delivers the thesis for the cost of voice lines and a few illustrations, but throws away the only sequence in the comic — the crater with Durand — that is genuinely better in first person than on paper.
>
> **The evidence is above. The ruling is the Summoner's.**

### The sniper: recurring antagonist, and the pages support it

The brief asked whether the pages support a **persistent hunter in an open AO** rather than a boss fight. **They do, emphatically, and they show you how:**
- He appears as **rumour** before he appears as a picture (I3 p8).
- He appears as a **silhouette the squad never notices** (I3 p13) — no reaction shot, no dialogue, the patrol just walks past a man who chose not to shoot.
- He appears as a **fragment of a face and a reticle** (I3 p20).
- He **takes a man** (I3 p22).
- Only at the end of 88 pages do you see him whole (I4 `fya.23`).

That is four escalation tiers before a face. In an open AO it needs no rails at all: a marksman entity that can be present in a patrol without ever engaging; that engages selectively; that occasionally **takes** rather than kills; whose lair is a findable place. The helmet wall is his kill counter, rendered as a prop.

### The four I would build first

1. **THE AMBUSH (I2 pp.18–23).** Wait in the grass, spring it, and then the aftermath: Michael vomiting, Gus praised for his kill, and one page later the ears. It is the comic's best sequence, it is entirely mechanical, and it contains the whole moral argument in dialogue that already exists.
2. **THE PATROL WATCHED (I3 p13).** A file crossing a stream with a scoped rifle in the foreground that never fires. This is one panel and it is the design document for the sniper. Build the *presence* before you build the fight.
3. **THE NIGHT PROBE ON THE WIRE (I1 pp.16–17).** Already the demo's shape. Add the flares and *"I swear I saw skeletons out in the bush"* — a man's report, with nothing behind it — and the comic's tonal rule is in the game at zero cost.
4. **THE SKULL-RINGED DUGOUT (I4 `fya.22`–`23`).** A place, not a boss. Find it, and find a wall of American helmets and a hammer-and-sickle flag in a hole in the ground. It answers a question the player has been carrying for hours without a single line of dialogue.

## 8 · WHAT THE GAME ALREADY HAS

**Already built and directly reusable:**
- **Hueys, insertions and extractions** — Issue 1's combat assault and Issue 2's cover are the game's existing loop.
- **A firebase** — sandbag revetments, huts, a tower, a mortar pit. Issue 3 p11's overhead is very nearly a screenshot.
- **NVA and VC** — including the porter columns of Issue 4 `fya.21`–`22`, which are a supply-line behaviour more than a combat one.
- **A squad system** — the comic's cast is a rifle squad with a leader, an officer, a friend and a problem.
- **A gore/gib system** — the comic is graphically violent in a specific, un-fun way, which suits a system that already exists.
- **PSX low-poly** — the heavy black-ink underground-comix look and a low-fidelity 3D renderer are natural allies. **Do not attempt to reproduce the linework in shaders**; reproduce the *staging*.
- **Jungle, elephant grass, streams, villages** — all present.

**Would need building:**
- **A named, persistent, non-boss marksman** who can observe without engaging, and who can abduct rather than kill.
- **A squadmate whose behaviour degrades over a campaign**, visibly, on his model — a necklace that appears, a bag he will not open. Eugene is a **character asset with states**, not a script.
- **Trophy-taking as a visible thing the AI does**, since both antagonists do it and the comic's whole argument is that they are the same act.
- **A tour clock / rotation counter.** *"Only 320 days left for ya?"* is a HUD element the comic has and the game does not.
- **The draft card as a character record.** Name, DOB, hometown, height, weight, eye colour, drawn as a period document — the author has already designed the game's character sheet, twice, on two title pages.
- **A rear-area HQ space** — infirmary, chow line, airfield, signpost. Issue 4 is largely set there.
- **WW1 everything**, *if* the Summoner rules WW1 playable (§7).
- **Ambient psy-op audio** for Wandering Soul.

**Explicitly out of scope, on the author's own ruling:** a stress/sanity/hallucination *system*. He has said the horror is *"not a main focus right now but that is the vibe of the comic."* It is the register the work is drawn in, not a mechanic to be implemented. Nothing above proposes one.

## 10 · WHAT IS NOT ON THE PAGE

Stated plainly so nobody fills these in later by accident.

- **The story stops.** The author: *"thats about as far of a story i had written overall."* It ends with Eugene screaming on a plane and the first full look at the sniper. **There is no ending. Do not write one.**
- **Issue 4 has no script.** 23 pages of finished ink and marker with **no dialogue and no captions**. The only text on them is lettering that is part of the art (**BLAM**, KILLED / ROTTEN / EATEN, INFIRMARY / HANOI / LONDON / NEW YORK, VICTOR CHARLES) and faint **pencil production notes in the margins** — "Detail Plants", "PLANTS", "eRase, Detail", and panel-placement notes reading "This goes…". Those are the artist talking to himself, not script.
- **READING DISCIPLINE, adopted after two of my own errors on the same page.** *Do not name what a drawing depicts when you can describe what the line does.* "BLAM" was read as an explosion because I named the effect before reading the letters; a weeping face was called melting because I named a genre before following the strokes. **In this book, marks running down a face are tears unless flesh is visibly coming apart.** Where a reading is uncertain, describe the mark and let the author say what it is.
- **CORRECTED 2026-09-09, on the author's word — "hes crying not melting."** The half-page face on `fya.12` is weeping, not dissolving. Every use of melting/running/dissolving applied to a face has been re-checked against the art and rewritten. **This is not a wording fix:** melting is a man becoming a monster, crying is a man staying human while doing something monstrous, and only the second is consistent with his own rule that the image measures the mind. See §5.
- **CORRECTED 2026-09-09.** An earlier draft of this bible said the shooting was absent from the pages. **That was wrong, and it was my misreading of `fya.12` — not a missing folder.** There is exactly one Issue 4 source folder (`issue 4\scans\`, 23 JPEGs) and it is the one that was read; no second edit exists. I read the "BLAM" as an explosion effect and the head-burst as a warping crowd. **The shooting is drawn** (§2, §5). A false negative is worse than a gap, because it tells the author something he made does not exist. Re-verified at full magnification.
- **The victim's rank is still not legible.** "The major" is the synopsis's word. What the page shows is a standing man in a soft bush hat.
- **The theft of the pistol is not drawn**, only the crowd, then the shot.
- **The nurse is drawn but unnamed.** She has no lines because no page in Issue 4 has lines. The freckles-to-pocks change on her face (§5) is a reading of the art, offered with its ambiguity stated, not a fact the text asserts.
- **WW2 does not appear anywhere in the four issues.** The SS officer, Russia, and the burning are a gap between the two drawn wars, not a third thread.
- **The female revolutionary is not drawn.** Neither is the bully's Foreign Legion career.
- **Champs' rank contradicts itself** across the erratum and Issue 3.
- **"Two months in-country" (I1 p4) and "320 days left" (I1 p19)** do not agree.
- **Crenshaw** may be a distinct soldier or a slip.
- **Pierre's resemblance to Eugene is drawn, never stated.**
- **Whether the horror is real is never answered**, and the one external corroboration the book allows itself is a nurse in 1915 confirming a corpse.
