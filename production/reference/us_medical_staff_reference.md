# US Medical Staff Reference — Field Medic, Surgeon/OR Team, Ward Staff
Vietnam ~1967-69. Compiled 2026-08-04 for FIELD MEDIC + HOSPITAL STAFF build.

**Sourcing key used throughout:** `SOURCED` = a cited source states this directly.
`ESTIMATED` = inferred from adjacent/general surgical-history sources, not a
Vietnam-specific citation — flagged, not presented as settled. `UNSOURCED —
NEEDS A RULING` = I could not find a citable answer; do not build to a guess,
ask Caleb or pick a documented-plausible option and say so explicitly.

Where period sources conflict, both are reported and flagged for his ruling —
none are silently resolved.

## 1. Surgical gown/apron — cut, colour, sleeve, tie-back

- **Colour, white vs green/blue, SOURCED but with a real period conflict:**
  - A dealer listing for an actual dated artifact — "VIETNAM ERA US Surgical
    Green Operating Gown, Medical Field Hospital, MASH, 1967" — places
    **surgical green** gowns in-country by 1967.
    [picclick listing](https://picclick.com/Vietnam-Era-US-Surgical-Green-Operating-Gown-Medical-192304159607.html)
  - Independently, general surgical-history sources (not Vietnam-specific)
    agree the trade shifted from white to green/blue **during the 1950s-60s**,
    for two stated reasons: less eye strain under bright OR lights after
    staring at red tissue, and better blood/stain masking. This corroborates
    the 1967 green gown as being in step with the wider trade, not an outlier.
    [ScrubHaven](https://www.scrubhaven.com/blogs/news/the-history-behind-scrub-colors-what-do-they-mean),
    [Columbia Surgery](https://columbiasurgery.org/news/habits-scrubs-brief-history-medical-and-surgical-dress)
  - Counter-datapoint, also SOURCED: multiple nurse first-person accounts of
    the era describe the **dress uniform** as white ("white nurse's uniform"
    with nylons, worn off-duty/in garrison), but explicitly say this was
    swapped for fatigues on actual duty — this is the SERVICE uniform, not OR
    attire, and should not be confused with it.
    [Working Nurse](https://www.workingnurse.com/articles/in-country-u-s-nurses-during-the-vietnam-war/)
  - **RULING NEEDED:** green is the better-evidenced OR gown colour for
    1967-69 (dated artifact + industry-wide trend both agree); white should
    be treated as the earlier/off-duty colour, not what the OR team operates
    in. Flagging rather than silently picking, per the "both sides" mandate —
    but on the weight of evidence, green is the stronger claim.
- **Apron over the gown, SOURCED as a real distinct garment, but NOT
  Vietnam-dated:** rubber/plastic waterproof aprons worn OVER the cloth gown
  in high-bleed procedures are a documented, still-current field-surgery item
  (MSF's own current medical catalog stocks "APRON SURGICAL, rubber" for
  exactly this purpose), and general surgical-attire sources describe them as
  added specifically where "a lot of bleeding... occurs."
  [MSF Unicat](https://unicat.msf.org/cat/product/13525),
  [Apprentice Doctor](https://theapprenticedoctor.com/the-protective-clothing-surgeons-wear/)
  **I could not find a Vietnam-1967-69-dated photo or account of this rubber
  apron specifically** — it is ESTIMATED that a busy evac-hospital OR handling
  high-velocity gunshot/frag wounds used the same rubber-apron-over-gown
  practice standard surgery already used, not invented. Flag as ESTIMATED.
- **Cut/length/sleeve/tie-back:** UNSOURCED — NEEDS A RULING. No source found
  gives Vietnam-specific cut measurements. Generic period surgical gowns of
  this era are knee-to-mid-calf length, back-tie/back-opening, long sleeves
  with a knit or tie cuff — this is standard period surgical-gown
  construction industry-wide (ESTIMATED from general surgical-attire history,
  not a Vietnam photo).

## 2. Surgical cap and mask

- **Cap styles present by the 1960s-70s, SOURCED (general surgical history,
  not Vietnam-specific):** bouffant (loose hairnet-style, full head coverage,
  commonly light blue), tie-back/"milkmaid" skull cap (snug, some ear/nape
  exposed), and surgical hoods. Cotton was the original material, giving way
  to nylon/polyester through the decade.
  [ResearchDive](https://www.researchdive.com/blog/a-guide-on-surgical-hats-definition-importance-and-types),
  [gotucoveredcaps.com](https://www.gotucoveredcaps.com/post/the-history-of-scrub-caps-in-healthcare)
  No Vietnam-specific photo confirming which style an evac-hospital OR used —
  ESTIMATED that the tie-back skull cap is the more period-plausible choice
  for a resource-limited field OR (cheaper, reusable, less material than a
  full bouffant), but this is a plausibility call, not a citation.
  **UNSOURCED — NEEDS A RULING** on which silhouette to model.
- **Mask coverage, SOURCED:** by the 1960s the standard cloth surgical mask
  covered "from the protrusion of the nose to under the chin," tied at the
  back of the head with cloth strings — i.e., nose+mouth+chin, NOT ears-only
  coverage. One source flags that mid-1960s masks did not always reliably
  cover the nose in practice even though the design intended to.
  [29Secrets history piece](https://29secrets.com/wellness/the-story-of-medical-and-fabric-face-masks/)
  Materials were cotton/gauze cloth, tied not elastic-looped, through this
  period; the ear-loop disposable mask is a later development beginning
  mid-1960s but not yet dominant. [same source]
  **For the model:** the mask should cover nose-to-chin, tie at the back of
  the head (2 tie points, upper + lower), leaving eyes and forehead/cap fully
  exposed for face-atlas readability (see Part 3 UV discussion).

## 3. Surgical gloves

- **Material/colour, SOURCED:** disposable latex surgical gloves were
  commercially available from 1964 (Ansell Rubber Company, gamma-sterilized).
  Natural latex of this era is a translucent pale amber/off-white, NOT
  bright white or blue-tinted (blue/purple nitrile dyeing is a much later,
  post-Vietnam development driven by latex-allergy visibility needs).
  [Past Medical History](https://www.pastmedicalhistory.co.uk/the-history-of-surgical-gloves/) —
  **note:** this exact page failed to re-fetch on a second pass (SSL error);
  citing from the successful first fetch's summary. Treat with slightly lower
  confidence and re-verify if this detail matters for final colour-picking.
- **Cuff length, ESTIMATED — flagged in the brief as something to source, and
  I could not find a period-specific citation.** General modern glove
  documentation confirms disposable surgical gloves run in longer "extended
  cuff" (~12", vs a 9" standard) variants specifically to protect the
  forearm — this is a documented modern spec, not proof of 1960s practice,
  but it is consistent with "gloves ran long, past the wrist" as stated in
  the brief. **UNSOURCED for the Vietnam-specific claim — treat "gloves
  extend a few inches past the wrist, tucked under the gown cuff" as a
  plausible, not verified, modelling choice.**

## 4. Bloodstaining — where it actually lands

**This is the detail Caleb named explicitly, and it is the weakest-sourced
item in this dossier. Do not model a pattern from imagination.**

- I found **no Vietnam-era photograph or firsthand account** that describes
  exactly where blood landed on a surgeon's gown/gloves/apron in this war.
  Searches for period OR photos (National Archives, Wikimedia Commons, the
  Texas Tech Vietnam Archive nurse exhibit, the Beth Parks 12th Evac
  first-person account) either returned no OR-interior images at all, or
  images that are portraits/ward shots without visible surgical staining.
  Direct evidence attempts and their results are logged so this isn't
  silently dropped:
  - TTU Vietnam Archive nurse exhibit — 11 images found, none show OR
    staining (ER desks, wards, IV changes, portraits).
    [exhibit page](https://www.vietnam.ttu.edu/exhibits/nurses/)
  - Beth Parks' firsthand 12th Evac account — describes overseeing OR Quonset
    construction, no clinical/staining detail.
    [bethparks.com](https://www.bethparks.com/12th-evacuation-hospital.html)
  - National Archives catalog search returned no OR-interior Vietnam records
    on the terms tried.
  - Wikimedia Commons category search for Vietnam military hospitals 404'd
    (category may not exist under that name, or is empty).
- **What general (non-Vietnam-specific) surgical-attire sources confirm, and
  which I am treating as ESTIMATED transferable fact, not invented:** "blood
  routinely covers gowns during surgery and often contaminates surgeons'
  undergarments and skin," and rubber aprons exist specifically because
  gowns alone don't stop soak-through in high-bleed cases.
  [PubMed abstract on gown barrier efficiency](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2039364/),
  [Apprentice Doctor](https://theapprenticedoctor.com/the-protective-clothing-surgeons-wear/)
- **RECOMMENDATION, explicitly flagged as inference, not citation:** anatomically
  the sources that DO exist agree staining concentrates where hands/arms work
  inside the wound and where gravity carries runoff — i.e. **forearms and
  glove cuffs** (constant contact), **apron front/lap at roughly hand-to-hip
  height** (where instruments and dressings are set down and where runoff
  drips), and low down/**shoe-cover level** (pooling on the floor under a
  litter). This is standard-surgery physical reasoning, not a Vietnam
  photograph. **Flagging this whole section for Caleb's ruling** — I would
  rather ship it as "best-inference, not verified" than silently present a
  specific splatter pattern as sourced fact. If he has reference of his own
  (the "Doc" footage/photos already used elsewhere in this project, or a war
  film he trusts), that should override this section.

## 5. The field medic ("Doc") — contrast with the OR team

- **NOT in surgical dress; in jungle fatigues, SOURCED generally** (VVMF,
  cherrieswriter firsthand account): medics carried an M16A1, .45 pistol,
  grenades, and "didn't have body armor or bulletproof helmets" — standard
  infantry gear plus the aid bag, not scrubs.
  [cherrieswriter.com](https://cherrieswriter.com/2024/02/24/what-it-was-really-like-as-a-medic-in-the-vietnam-war/),
  [VVMF](https://www.vvmf.org/topics/Medics-and-Corpsmen/)
- **Red-cross brassard/marking removed in the field — SOURCED, multiple
  independent statements, consistent with each other:**
  - "During the Vietnam War the medic looked like any other Joe... no
    distinguishing marks, markings on his helmet nor on his arms."
    [212warriors.com](https://www.212warriors.com/army_medic.html) (explicitly
    a personal-memoir style page, not a documented military history —
    weight accordingly)
  - A WebSearch-aggregated summary states medics "were the first targets of
    the North Vietnamese, especially when they were with recon teams," and
    would "remove his identifying marks as soon as they left the base camp."
    I was not able to open the underlying page directly to re-verify the
    exact wording — **treat this specific "first targets" phrasing as
    ESTIMATED/aggregated, not a direct quote I confirmed myself.**
  - This matches **the project's own standing ruling**, already recorded and
    already built into the codebase: `tools/make_medic.py:317-321` states
    plainly, in the owner's own words as recorded there, "NO RED CROSS ON THE
    FIELD MEDIC. Ruled by the owner, and it is what the archival footage
    shows: not one medic in any frame wears a brassard or a helmet cross,
    because the marking made him the first man shot." That is a stronger,
    more specific source than anything I found today (Caleb reviewed actual
    footage) — **defer to it.**
- **Aid bag:** the M5/M3 canvas aid bag, already modelled in-repo
  (`tools/make_medic.py`, `tools/make_satchel.py`), ~30×20×12 cm per
  `make_medic.py:31` citing the M3 bag dimension. Not re-verified against an
  external source today since it is already a built, working asset — flagging
  that this dimension traces to a prior session, not to a source checked in
  this pass.

## 6. Triage/ward staff — nurses and corpsmen vs the OR team

- **SOURCED, general:** ward/ER nursing staff are shown in these sources in
  standard uniform or fatigues, NOT surgical gowns — e.g. "1st Lieutenant
  Cheri Hawes... at desk for Emergency Room, 91st Evacuation Hospital" and
  "Lieutenant Magnolia Lilly... in Emergency Room" — both captioned as
  desk/ward duty, not OR, and neither caption mentions surgical dress.
  [TTU Vietnam Archive nurse exhibit](https://www.vietnam.ttu.edu/exhibits/nurses/)
  A corroborating firsthand account: "In practice, Army nurses usually wore
  fatigues" for day-to-day ward work, reserving white dress uniform for
  off-duty/formal occasions.
  [Working Nurse](https://www.workingnurse.com/articles/in-country-u-s-nurses-during-the-vietnam-war/)
- **Women present in-theater, SOURCED, and what it means for silhouette:**
  Army Nurse Corps women served in every evac hospital cited above (Cu Chi,
  Chu Lai, Tuy Hoa, etc.) — confirmed by name and photo caption in the TTU
  archive. **If a female ward-staff or nurse figure is ever built, that is a
  distinct silhouette/rig requirement** (different base proportions, hair,
  possibly a distinct base mesh) and is explicitly out of scope for today's
  medic/surgeon/gore-compatible male grunt-derived builds — flagging so it
  is not accidentally treated as "just re-skin the same base."
- **The project's own existing "tent doctor" build already made a
  contrasting call** (see Part 2 below): plain shirt, no mask, bare-headed —
  this predates today's brief and is now in direct tension with Caleb's new
  instruction. Reported, not resolved, in Part 3.

## 7. What a Vietnam field-hospital OR looked like — staging context

- **SOURCED, sparse:** Beth Parks (RN, oversaw OR construction at the 12th
  Evac, Cu Chi, 1966) states the new OR Quonsets could handle "seven wounded
  men at one time instead of the three we could accommodate at the 7th
  Surg" — confirms **multiple operating tables in one open Quonset hut bay**,
  not single-table private theaters.
  [bethparks.com](https://www.bethparks.com/12th-evacuation-hospital.html)
- **Casualty flow, SOURCED (US Army official history):** "Patients were moved
  from the helicopter pad directly into the preoperative and resuscitation
  shelter where they were met by the surgical team on-call" — confirms a
  helipad → pre-op/resus → OR pipeline, consistent with the project's
  existing litter/dustoff asset chain
  (`assets/us/vehicles/heli_casualty_load.glb`,
